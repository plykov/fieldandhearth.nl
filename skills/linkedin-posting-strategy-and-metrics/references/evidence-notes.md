# Evidence notes: how far to trust the source

The ideas in these three skills come from a long report titled as an analysis of 54,187 viral LinkedIn posts using data science and an AI assistant. Use it as a well-organized synthesis of common creator wisdom, not as rigorous research.

## Why caution
- **Unclear method.** The report does not show the dataset, the sampling, the statistical tests or the code. Its p-values cannot be checked.
- **Blended sources.** Many figures are borrowed from other studies or newsletters and presented next to the report's own findings, for example the 31.7% subjectivity effect, the 27/18/17/10% framework shares, and the 70% higher engagement for image posts without links.
- **Small-sample claims stated as rules.** "9 out of 10 top posts use I, me, my or you" comes from a handful of top posts. Almost every post uses those words, so the claim has little discriminating power.
- **Implausible number.** A best length of 1,900 to 2,000 words refers to a different format (likely articles) and conflicts with the 3,000-character post limit.
- **Survivorship bias.** It studies posts that already went viral. Many unsuccessful posts used the same hooks, formats and timing, so these patterns are necessary-looking, not proven sufficient.
- **Platform drift.** Algorithms change. Links, polls, carousels and timing advice age quickly.
- **Mixed ethics.** The report mentions rallying friends for early likes, comment-to-get tactics and fake-ish urgency. These skills reject manufactured engagement and require honest delivery.

## What holds up well
Hooks and previews matter because the feed truncates posts. Conversational personal writing, short paragraphs, specifics and a useful takeaway are widely observed. Keeping value inside the post and replying quickly to comments are low-risk habits.

## How to use the numbers
Quote them only as "one analysis reported" and never as guaranteed effects. Prefer the user's own analytics after about 10 to 20 posts.

## Second report: 850 top-decile posts (client-attraction analysis)
Deeper per-post schema (40+ variables, language-model enrichment) but a narrower base: the top 10% of posts from a curated group of expert creators, many of whom sell courses, books or services via LinkedIn.
- **No baseline.** Without ordinary posts for comparison, shares such as "62% of hooks contain a number" show what these creators do, not what drives results.
- **Selection and survivorship.** Creators were chosen because they succeed; we cannot see the many who tried the same moves.
- **Commercial incentive.** The author promotes related products, and the creators profit from comment-to-get funnels. Treat ethics advice (transparency, delivery) as additions, not as findings.
- **Some quoted examples are paraphrased or unverifiable.** Do not reuse their numbers.
- **What is stronger:** the qualitative structure (value-first posts, hook-to-payoff, light CTAs, topic consistency) agrees with the first report and with common practice.
