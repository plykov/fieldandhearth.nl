# Reading narrative-driven growth critically (paraphrased; for analysis, not for copying)

## Why this is in a teardown toolkit
Two Origin Notes pieces examine growth that moved valuation more than revenue: Instinct (engineered scarcity and a seeded testimonial chorus) and a separate essay on the "fear" playbook. The author explicitly does not recommend copying the fear play; the value here is spotting it.

## Incentive check (the Munger question)
Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome. For any claim or launch chorus ask: who benefits if I believe it? What do the speakers own (stakes, allocations, advisory roles, sponsorship)? What is verified versus claimed? What would falsify it, and by when?

## Fear / grand-claim narrative: four conditions the author lists for it to work
1. The threat is unmeasurable (no test date; claims with deadlines such as Y2K die on the date).
2. You are the authority on the thing you warn about.
3. The danger points back at your own product, so fear equals power.
4. The audience holds capital or writes rules.
Effects the author describes: higher valuation; justification for staying private; displaced accountability (words like "emergent" or "inevitable" move blame away from builders); regulatory moats that fall hardest on small and open-source rivals. Caveat: it does not prove the danger is fake; a warning can be sincere, self-serving and correct at once.

## Manufactured belief (Instinct)
Signals to examine: access gated to authorities; identical testimonial structure; one launch slogan across outlets; no independently verified users, retention or revenue; terms that grant sweeping rights. Judge by evidence that survives scrutiny, not by chorus size.

Add a section "What is verified, what is claimed, who benefits" with source labels (C/P/E/Q) and a note on survivorship bias.
